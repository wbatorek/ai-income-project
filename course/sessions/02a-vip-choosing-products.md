## 1. Summary

A digital product is anything **developed digitally and delivered digitally**. It's a "recipe, not a cake": you build it once and sell it any number of times at almost no delivery cost.
Formats: ebook, digital assets, pre-recorded programs and "digital minds". Start with the simplest (an ebook) and add complexity only once that sells.
Value is **not** measured in pages or content: buyers pay for a **transformation** from point A to point B. A 20-page or even 5-page action plan can be worth more than 200 pages.
Whether a product sells is judged on **five "digital product unlocks"**:
- **pain**
- **worsening pain**
- **transformation**
- **specificity**
- **speed**

Pain is "90% of the way there". Choose pain over pleasure, and judge it by **how often the person is reminded of it**.
The output is a one-sentence, four-part **product promise**: *who + painful (worsening) problem + result + time*.
The AI tool they demo in the main session reportedly scores product ideas on exactly these five criteria.
VIP homework: spot a problem around you, draft a product idea, test it against the five unlocks and post it in the VIP group.

## 3. The selection framework: the five digital product unlocks

He describes them as "the main gears" of their AI product builder (built "around two years ago"). The AI "judges which digital product is better than the other one" by scoring each idea on these five. "If you can have one that scores high in every single one … you have a very, very good digital product."

> Numbering slip in the talk: before the unlocks he says the 20-page ebook beats the 200-page one "because of the **fourth** unlock". Later he explains that with **speed**, which is the fifth. He also says "unlock number five" just before covering specificity (the fourth). The order on his recap slide is pain → worsening pain → transformation → specificity → speed.

### Unlock 1: Pain (≈ min 23–30). "The most important question"

**Definition:** the specific pain point the product removes. "If your product doesn't fix any pain point, no one's going to buy it."

**Pain vs pleasure:**
- Problems are either pain-related or pleasure-related (new phone, PS5, car, better photos).
- Claim: people move **away from pain about 2× more strongly** than toward pleasure. If you do nothing about a want, life stays comfortable. If a dog is biting your leg, you move.
- So pick pain problems, not pleasure problems.

**Three pillars where pains live:**
- **wealth**
- **health**
- **relationships**

**Hobbies** are a weaker fourth pillar because they are usually pleasure ("how much does it hurt not to take good pictures? Probably not a lot").

**How to judge or measure it: reminder frequency.**
- "The more often you're reminded of that problem … during your week, especially during your day, the more painful that is."
- Toothache: reminded every time you eat, talk, work or try to sleep.
- Teeth whitening: noticed once or twice a week.

**Examples:**

| Bad / weak | Good / strong |
|---|---|
| Guide to cutting watermelons. Almost nobody has the problem, and those who do wouldn't pay | Toothache at 2 a.m.: you'll fix it "in two, three days, I guarantee you" |
| Teeth whitening before summer pictures (a little pain) | |
| Better photography (hobby or pleasure) | |
| **Weight loss**: a contrarian take. It's commonly thought the most profitable niche, but you're only reminded "a few times a week, maybe once a day" (jeans, photos) and "got used to it very quickly" | |

**Other points:**
- **The idea is almost worthless.** You don't need a "new telephone" or Uber-style invention. Choose "a very simple idea that people already think about … proven and simple". "As long as humans have problems, the demand for digital products is always going to be there."
- **"90% rule":** "If we find a painful problem to solve and a good solution for it, you can really get everything else wrong … and you're still going to make money."

### Unlock 2: Worsening pain (≈ min 30–34)

**Definition:** how much worse the pain gets over time, and how fast. "The worse it gets and the faster it gets worse, the more desirable the solution." It peaks at the point where "you just got to fix that problem". **That's urgency.**

**How to judge it:** picture the pain's curve over time, flat or rising. Look for escalating stages and an approaching breaking point.
- Toothache stages: sensitive → dull ache → throbbing → can't eat or sleep → call the dentist.
- A sign of worsening in a description: the person has *started* to avoid things ("started sitting out games").

**Examples:**

| Flat / slow (weak) | Rising fast (strong) |
|---|---|
| Overweight: the consequences build over "years and years" | Toothache gets worse daily, even within the day |
| Wanting white teeth: stays at the same level | |
| A chip in the car windscreen: annoying, but doesn't get worse | Faulty car battery leaves you stuck 40 min from home: "I'm buying a new battery **today**" |

**Dependency:** this mostly follows from choosing the right pain ("if you choose the wrong pain, it's kind of hard for it to get worse"). Still, use it as a separate filter.

### Unlock 3: Transformation (≈ min 34–42)

**Definition:** buyers pay for going **from point A (current painful state) to point B (ideal end state)**, not for pages, megabytes or length. "They're buying something that you cannot see on screen."

**Method (how to judge or build it):**
1. **Map point A first.** Point B then becomes obvious. Point A includes:
   - **how they feel**
   - **what they fear**
   - **what they avoid**
   - **how they see themselves**
2. Define point B as the ideal end state in the same terms (feelings, behaviour, identity).
3. Every pain has its own transformation, even inside one topic. In football, "shooting with more power" and "playing without knee pain" are different problems, solutions and buyers. In parenting, each parent's ideal evening routine is different.

**Worked example (football knee pain):**
- **Point A:** knee hurts after every match; worried they'll have to give up football; have *started sitting out games* (a sign of worsening); used to be on the main squad; think "their playing days are over".
- **Point B:** play a full match without pain; back in the team; "see themselves as a player again".

**Key test, the "articulation" principle:** "If you can get your digital product to articulate their problem better than they can articulate their own problem themselves, they're pretty much sold." Once you describe their pain that well, the buyer assumes you must have the solution, and "it doesn't really matter what solution you propose".

**Why pay when information is free?** Free information is scattered and contradictory: 100 YouTube videos and many books.
The product hands over a ready action plan "on a silver platter" and removes the research, thinking and doubt.
- The presenter's own story: he read about 20 fitness books, ended up more confused, bought a meal and workout plan, and found the 5 pages worth more than hundreds of pages.
- His mindset point: creators have a "scarcity mindset" and keep adding content. "Most of the times, it's not about adding more. It's actually about keeping it simple."
- In the main session the AI is said to map point A, point B and "step one, two, three, four, five" between them.

### Unlock 4: Specificity (≈ min 44–48)

**Definition:** target a narrow, clearly identified person with a clearly identified problem.
Small sellers can do what big companies can't: Nike has to sell to the masses, so it can't build a shoe for one type of person with one problem.

**Narrowing ladder:**
- **parents** ("okay, but not very good")
- → **dads / moms** ("50% less people, so 100% more specific")
- → **divorced dads with teenage sons** ("way more specific … way better")

**Why it works:** the right person feels "you were talking to me". That produces the articulation effect from unlock 3.

**Boundary rule (verbatim):** "specific enough that the right person feels it, but common enough that plenty of people have that problem." Don't narrow to one city or a micro-group. "Divorced dads with teenage sons … there's still probably millions and millions of those."

**How to judge it:** the narrowing must come from a **distinguishing characteristic or problem**, not an adjective that applies to everyone in the group.

| Specific (good) | Fake-specific (bad) |
|---|---|
| "New moms building repeatable newborn bedtimes without rigid schedules": a specific person and a specific pain | "Busy moms dropping fat fast with daily 30-minute fat-burning routines". "Every single mom is busy", so "busy" narrows nothing |
| "Dual-working parents cooking complete dinners without (even) chopping": both parents work full days and still need dinner | |

### Unlock 5: Speed (≈ min 48–50)

**Definition:** for the same result, the faster route to it is more valuable.
This is why a 20-page ebook beats a 200-page one when both give the same solution.

**Examples:**
- Dentist: fixed in 5 minutes vs 5 hours. You want 5 minutes, and "what matters is, is the pain fixed or not?"
- Results in 6 months vs 2 months: choose 2.
- He calls the belief that you must work hard or produce a lot to justify the price "another limiting belief".

**Caveat: realism.** "Lose 10 kilos in two days" isn't believable. "If it's not realistic, then people are not going to believe it … and then it doesn't matter." Faster only helps if it stays credible.

## 4. How buyers decide what to buy (psychology as taught)

1. **Moving away from pain beats moving toward pleasure (claimed ~2×).** With pleasure, doing nothing is comfortable. With pain, staying put is unbearable.
2. **Reminder frequency is the felt intensity.** Several reminders a day (eating, talking, sleeping, working) means high intent. Weekly reminders mean the buyer "gets used to it".
3. **Urgency comes from escalation.** A worsening pain reaches a breaking point where the buyer decides to buy *today* ("I don't know which battery I'm going to buy, but I'm going to buy a battery"). The decision to buy comes *before* the choice of which product.
4. **They buy the end state, not the artefact.** They judge by the transformation they can *picture*, not by size or length. "If your digital product can get them to visualise this transformation … that's what they're buying."
5. **Recognition triggers trust.** If you describe their problem better than they can, they assume you have the solution. Specific targeting creates this "you're talking to me" effect.
6. **They pay for convenience and certainty.** Free information exists, but it's contradictory and takes work. Buyers pay to skip research and doubt ("Am I doing the right thing or not?").
7. **Faster is better, if it's believable.** Less time and effort to get the result raises value. An unrealistic speed claim destroys belief.
8. **The creator's credentials don't matter.** "People don't care who you are … It's related to, does it fix the problem that I have?" (Said about the budget tracker, whose creator is unknown.)
9. **Problems are framed by life pillar.** Wealth, health and relationships pains convert. Hobbies are usually pleasure, so they convert less.

## 5. The product promise (four-part formula, ≈ min 51)

"It's a sentence with four parts":
1. **Who**: a specific person
2. **Problem**: a painful problem (ideally one that's getting worse)
3. **Result**: point B, the transformation
4. **Time**: how long it takes to get there

**Template (as spoken):**
> "I help **[specific person]** with **[specific problem that's getting worse]** get **[point B / final transformation]** in **[time]**."

**The only example given:**
> "I help **first-time parents of 4 to 6 month olds** **whose nights get harder every week** get **their baby sleeping through the night** in **seven nights**."

Notice that the worsening is written *into* the problem clause ("get harder every week"). One sentence carries all five unlocks:
- specificity (first-time parents, 4–6 month olds)
- pain (sleepless nights)
- worsening (harder every week)
- transformation (sleeping through the night)
- speed (seven nights)

The other example titles from section 3 (newborn bedtimes, dual-working-parent dinners) were shown as product *titles*, not full promises.

## 9. Claims vs. evidence

**Taught principles.** These are reasonable and mostly match standard direct-response copywriting (Schwartz/Halbert-style pain, specificity and "enter the conversation in their head"). None was backed by data in the session.

| Principle | Assessment |
|---|---|
| Developed + delivered digitally = digital product; recipe vs cake economics | A definition. Sound |
| Pain > pleasure, "2×" | Loosely borrowed from **loss aversion** (Kahneman & Tversky, ~2× for losses vs gains), which isn't the same as pain vs pleasure in buying. The direction is plausible; the "2×" figure is rhetoric |
| Reminder frequency as a pain measure | A useful heuristic, not evidence. The "weight loss isn't that painful" take contradicts how big the weight-loss market obviously is. Treat it as a rule for ranking niches *for small sellers*, not a market truth |
| Worsening = urgency | Standard copywriting principle. Plausible |
| Transformation > length; short action plans beat long content | Plausible. Supported only by the presenter's own anecdote |
| Articulate the problem better than the buyer can | Classic copywriting maxim. "It doesn't really matter what solution you propose" is an overstatement and a refund/ethics risk |
| Specific but common enough; avoid fake-specific adjectives | Sound and practical. The best-explained unlock |
| Faster is better if realistic | Sound. The realism caveat is important for platform rules and refunds |
| Four-part product promise | A useful template. Unproven, but cheap to test |

**Unverified income claims and sales framing:**

## 10. Implications for our system: draft niche/product scoring model

> **⚠ OUR INTERPRETATION, NOT COURSE CONTENT.** Below we turn the five unlocks into a rubric an AI research and scoring agent can apply. We add the criteria the course left out (ability to pay, reach, competition, verifiability, effort, risk), because the five unlocks only measure *desirability*, not *feasibility* or *distribution*. Weights are starting guesses and should be adjusted later with real conversion and refund data from the analytics agent (see research doc §3.2/§3.6).

### 10.1 Structure

```
Idea = (segment, problem, point A, point B, time-to-result, format)

Desirability (course "five unlocks")   – 60%
Feasibility & business (our additions) – 40%

Hard gates (reject regardless of total):
  Pain < 3  |  Specificity < 3  |  Verifiability < 3  |  Policy/claim risk = 1
```

**Total score** = Σ(score × weight), normalised to 0–100.

**Suggested thresholds:** ≥ 75 → recommend at gate G1; 60–74 → "needs evidence"; < 60 or any gate failure → discard, but log the reason so the rubric can learn.

### 10.3 What the research agent should output per idea (machine-checkable)
- `segment` (with distinguishing attribute), `pillar`, `problem`, `point_A{feel, fear, avoid, self_image}`, `point_B{...}`, `time_to_result`, `format`
- **Draft product promise** in the course's template: *"I help [who] with [worsening problem] get [result] in [time]."*
- **Evidence pack:** at least 5 verbatim quotes with URLs, community list with sizes and promo rules, 3–10 competitor listings with prices and review counts, keyword/size proxies
- **Per-criterion score + one-line justification + confidence (low/med/high)**. Low-confidence scores are flagged for the human.
- "Why it might fail" (already in our G1 design)

### 10.4 Automatic vs. human split (summary)
- **Automatable (agent):**
  - harvesting complaint quotes and counting frequency
  - intensity and temporal-language tagging
  - segment-size proxies
  - competitor and price scraping within ToS
  - fake-specificity check
  - A/B mapping draft
  - promise drafting
  - policy keyword screen
- **Human (operator at G1):**
  - realism of speed and result claims
  - whether quotes represent real, common pain
  - verifiability and personal fit
  - ethics/claims
  - final price
  - the articulation test with real people (cheapest real validation: post the promise or problem statement in a community and see whether people say "that's me")

### 10.5 Illustrative promises in the operator's niches (unvalidated, for testing the rubric only)
- *"I help NetSuite admins whose go-live is less than 30 days away and whose saved searches keep timing out get a clean, documented search library in one weekend."* Wealth/career pillar; worsening via deadline; B2B ability to pay; high verifiability.
- *"I help home-studio podcasters whose episodes get listener complaints about muddy audio get broadcast-ready vocals in one afternoon with a ready-made vocal-chain template."* Weaker on pain intensity (closer to a hobby). Run D1 honestly.

### 10.6 Course-to-system mapping notes
- "Pain is 90%" → in practice **D1 and the D1 gate dominate**. Over-weighting it beyond 20% risks favouring health niches (high pain, high claim risk). The F6 gate offsets that.
- "Start simple" → the creation agent's default format is **ebook / checklist / template**. Higher formats only after sales data exists (Phase 3 in the research doc).
- "Transformation, not length" → the QA agent should **penalise padding**. Target the shortest asset that delivers point B, and measure it with an "actionable steps per page" heuristic.
- The course's own AI reportedly scores ideas on the same five criteria. Our model is a **superset**, which lets us compare our rankings with theirs if we see their tool in the main session.
